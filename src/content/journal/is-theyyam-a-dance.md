## Why it gets called a dance

A Theyyam is vivid. There is the sound of drums, a tall headdress, painted face and layered attire, and movement that can be fast, forceful or slow and grave. If you do not know what is happening, the closest familiar word is “dance”.

Language adds to it. Many ritual names, such as *kaliyattam* and *vellattam*, end in *attam*, a word that is often translated as dance or movement. Travel writing then repeats the translation, and the idea spreads.

## What the movements are

In a Theyyam the movements are steps of a ritual. They follow patterns that belong to a particular deity, shrine and family, and they are learned through years of training. Some sources describe sets of ritual footwork, called *kalasams*, that a deity goes through. They are not chosen for display. They are part of how the deity appears, blesses and is honoured.

## What the koladhari does

The koladhari takes the *kolam*, the form of the deity. Before the ritual there is preparation: rules to keep, often fasting, and many hours of face painting and dressing in the green room. When the headdress is placed, the koladhari is believed to become the deity. See [The steps of a Theyyam](steps-of-a-theyyam.html) for the stages in order.

From then on the deity is addressed as a deity. Devotees bow, make offerings and ask for blessings. The deity speaks to them, listens to troubles and may answer questions or settle disputes. That is why many people use the word *worship* for what takes place.

## Who is present

Those present are devotees, families and neighbours, together with the people whose task it is to serve the ritual. A visitor is a guest in that gathering. This is not an event arranged for onlookers, and many Theyyams are family occasions held at a family shrine.

:::quote
“For the length of the ritual, the village speaks to the god.”
:::

## The words to use

If you write or talk about Theyyam, a few small changes show respect:

- Say **ritual**, not dance or show.
- Say that the Theyyam **appears** or **is held**, not that it is staged.
- Say **koladhari** for the person who takes the kolam, not artist or actor.
- Say **attire**, not costume.
- Say **devotees** or **those present**, not audience.

## What this means for you as a visitor

Treat it as you would any act of worship. Dress modestly, keep your voice low, do not clap or call out, and never step into the ritual space unless invited. Photography and video are banned at some Theyyams, so ask before taking out a phone. Read [Cameras and ritual](cameras-and-ritual.html) for how to handle this.
